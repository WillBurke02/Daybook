Production and recycling of steel

Introduction

As the concept of sustainable development becomes more relevant to our society it is important to inspect and understand the processes which have the greatest environmental impacts. In 2024 the world consumed ca.100 billion tonnes of material which equates to over 12 tonnes of materials per person per year. The most-consumed materials (by mass) are those used in the construction industry, including concrete, wood, and steels, each exceeding 1 billion tonnes annually.

Steel in particular garners interest for a few reasons. Considering mass of materials consumed and embodied energy, production of steel is the most environmentally costly; at the same time, secondary production (recycling) of metals in general is far less energy intensive compared to primary production. A significant positive impact can be made if the production and recycling of steel is improved.

This TLP contains a brief introduction to life cycle analysis, followed by discussion on current methods of steel production, and outlines some aspects of recycling both steels and other materials in general.

Note on terminology Depending on the type and grade of steel in production, and the scope in consideration, different stages of production may be referred to by different terms. Primary production as referred to in this TLP corresponds the primary production stage in LCA and includes all production stages from ores to product steel, which encompasses ore processing, primary steelmaking, and secondary steelmaking.

Sometimes, secondary steelmaking is also used to refer to the recycling of steel or the EAF process (when charged mostly with scrap). As seen in the following sections, this TLP uses secondary production to describe the recycling of steel, in accordance with LCA concepts.

Life Cycle Analysis (LCA)

For any given product or material, its possible recycling routes of are heavily dependent on production decisions. An environmentally friendly production processes may lead to various complications including limited recycling option; similarly, aiming only for high recyclability may result in complicated production process or compromised product functionalities. It is therefore important, when considering production and recycling, to look at the entire product lifetime together.

The life of a product can be roughly separated into four stages, each with associated embodied energies and CO2 emissions.

  1. Creation of material: extraction or production of raw materials
  2. Manufacture: turning raw materials into the product
  3. Use: distribution and maintenance
  4. Waste disposal: recycle, reuse, landfill, or combustion

Optimistically, waste after the useful life can be recycled and reformed into new materials or new products, completing the cycle.

Life cycle analysis (LCA) aims to track resources and emissions associated with each stage of the cycle, in attempt to quantify environmental impacts. There are a few factors which must be decided when carrying out LCA, in order to fairly evaluate the exact product or service.

Note life cycle analysis and life cycle assessment are used synonymously.

The LCA approach is not without drawbacks, notably:

  • The calculated results from LCA typically have large uncertainties, as the data used are taken from industry averages rather than specific to the scenario, and uses various assumptions
  • The analysis is carried out post-design, meaning the results are not directly helpful in the design process
  • LCA is an exhaustive and complete analysis, which can be laborious and slow
  • The output of complete LCA may require specialist knowledge for interpretation.

To overcome the complexity, streamlined LCA is sometimes carried out. The matrix method provides a simple and flexible method for scoring environmental impact but is only quasi-quantitative, and the scoring process relies heavily on knowledge and experience.

An alternative approach is the so called "eco-audit", which focuses on two representative outputs, CO2 emission and embodied energy, instead of the full array of results from LCA. This simplifies the process as the data required are commonly reported, and the outcome of eco-audits are still quantitative enough for efficiently comparing different products.

Energy and emission savings from using recycled material is accounted for at the input stage of each life cycle. To avoid double counting these savings potential benefits from product disposal choices (recycling, reusing, etc.) are not considered. These benefits are known as the end-of-life (EOL) potentials, and are not included in the lifetime calculation; visually, EOL potentials are represented by hashed bars in eco-auditing software such as Granta EduPack

Even in the simple eco-audit example above, amount of recycled steel used affects environmental impact of the production stage, and choosing to reuse the product leads to EOL potentials which are dependent on production decisions. Real-life life cycles are often far more complicated, and it is important to study various production and recycling options with the co-dependencies in mind.

Primary production: ore processing

The initial feedstock for the production of iron is called ore, which are compounds of the element of interest. In the case of iron this is mostly Fe2O3. which is reduced to Fe during primary production.

Historically, this is achieved by heating ores with charcoal. Provided the airflow is sufficient, the burning charcoal provides heat for various chemical reactions, and forms CO needed for reduction.

The Fe-rich initial product of reduction from ores is known as iron, raw iron, or pig iron. The “iron” produced here still contains high C content, as well as non-negligible amounts of other impurities.

Widely used modern techniques for primary ore processing include the blast furnace process and direct reduction of iron (DRI).

Blast furnace

Today, the majority of ore processing around the world is carried out in blast furnaces.

The blast furnace process relies on the same reactions as historical production routes. Coke is added instead of charcoal, which itself is produced by heating coal in absence of air. Blasts of O2 react with C in coke to form CO.

Ores, coke, and limestone are added to the blast furnace from the top. The minerals slowly move down the furnace and face increasingly high temperatures. Ores are reduced by the rising CO and forms molten elemental Fe and gaseous CO2. As melting iron consumes significant amounts of thermal energy, the iron produced from the blast furnace is often immediately transported in ladles (refractory-lined containers) to steelmaking processes. Limestone thermally decomposes to form CaO, which reacts with other impurities (SiO2, etc.) to form the slag phase, which, due to the high temperature, is also molten.

Iron produced from the blast furnace is typically known as pig iron.

Direct reduced iron (DRI)

In DRI, reactions occur between solid ores and reducing gas. In comparison to the blast furnace, this uses significantly less heat as there is no need for melting the ores, however it is also less efficient at removing impurities. As a result, relatively high-quality ores are required for DRI.

As of 2025, the industry standard still uses reducing gas mixture derived from natural gas, which, just like the blast furnace, produces huge amounts of CO2 and other greenhouse gases. New industrial developments include the use of green H2 as the reducing gas , which produces H2O as the oxidation product instead. Note however that current industrial processes are designed for iron with some C content, whereas iron from H2 DRI would not have this C content; furthermore, there may be potential issues with hydrogen embrittlement.

DRI iron is known as sponge iron due to its appearance and is solid as the DRI process takes place below the melting point of iron.

Other techniques include electrolysis . While this is a novel area for steel production it is common practise for aluminium.

Primary production: primary steelmaking

Steels typically contain less than 2% C, while iron obtained from reduction of ores contain up to 4% C, due to the coke and natural gas used. Steelmaking uses O2 to burn off the excess C, forming CO in the process.

Comparing to ore reduction, the chemistry here is much more precise. To control the reactions, the steelmaking furnaces are charged with calculated ratio of iron and fluxes, and high purity O2 is used. Some recycling is done at this stage, as the steelmaking processes are effective in removing impurities, and the incorporation of scrap in the charge also helps control the composition in the furnace.

Steelmaking is typically carried out in the basic oxygen furnace or the electric arc furnace. In contrast to ore reduction processes, the steelmaking processes are not continuous.

Basic oxygen steelmaking (BOS)

The molten pig iron produced in blast furnaces can be transferred directly to basic oxygen steelmaking (BOS). No heat is supplied to the basic oxygen furnace (BOF), as the steelmaking reactions are exothermic and generate enough heat to maintain the furnace temperature. Cold scrap is sometimes added for temperature control.

O2 is blown into the furnace via a water-cooled lance. The O2 flow has very high pressure and velocity, which effectively stirs the charge. Addition of fluxes helps removal of impurities; a slag phase forms and floats on top of the steel and is foamed due to fast air flow in the furnace. The foamed slag then acts as thermal insulation.

Note that acronyms BOS and BOF are often used interchangeably to refer to the process.

Electric arc furnace

An electric arc furnace (EAF) is charged with solid material. The iron used in an EAF may be sponge iron (from DRI), scrap, or a mixture of both.

Three graphite electrodes (arranged in a triangle when viewed from the top) are used to generate electric arcs which melt the charge. Small fuel burners are used to heat the cold spots and supply the O2 required for steelmaking.

Thermal efficiency is an important consideration in EAF. Some design choices are made to reduce heat loss, for example foaming the slag for thermal insulation, and pre-heating the charge with hot waste gas.

The DRI/EAF route is gaining more interest due to the relatively lower environmental impact, and the high capacity for recycling.

Primary production: secondary steelmaking

Crude steel produced in primary steelmaking processes is often cast and used as-is, especially for applications construction or automobile industries. In these user scenarios, the exact physical and chemical properties of steel is relatively unimportant.

Some applications have higher requirements for specific properties, and lower tolerance for variation between batches. These higher-grade steels are produced from crude steels via secondary steelmaking processes, which further removes impurities and adjust compositions to suit specific requirements.

Secondary steelmaking processes require high level of control over the melt and the surrounding environment, making them economically and energetically expensive. As different impurities affect the final microstructure and properties differently, they are targeted selectively during the making of different types and grades of steel; accordingly, the refining approaches vary. Some typical refining processes include:

Deoxidation, desulfurisation , and decarburisation Reactive elements are removed by reaction with added reagents. The reaction products favour a different phase (slag, air, etc.) to steel.

Vacuum degassing Volatile elements (notably H2) and gaseous inclusions can be removed from the molten steel by lowering the surrounding pressure.

Flotation Some inclusions are less dense than steel and naturally float, forming the slag phase. Small inclusions can also be removed by blowing gas through the melt; the inclusions attach to gas bubbles and float up to the slag phase.

Dilution Non-reactive elements (such as Cu in steel) are extremely difficult to remove. While technologies such as electrolysis are theoretically feasible, they are extremely expensive and rarely available. More often, Cu-rich melts are diluted with higher-purity steel, in order to reduce the Cu content below certain thresholds.

Another important aspect of secondary steelmaking is the addition of alloying elements. Alloying additions are made to alter certain physical, chemical, and microstructural changes; a common example is the addition of Cr in stainless steel, in order to form a protective chromia layer and protect the Fe from oxidising. These alloying elements are added in precise quantities, similar to how the refining reagents are added.

Recycling: overview

In short, recycling of metals involves recovering waste material (known as scrap) and reinserting it back in the primary production process. Compared to primary production, this secondary production does not require ore processing and is therefore far less energetically and economically costly.

The challenge is to determine the appropriate point of re-entry. In steel production, scrap most commonly re-enters production at primary steelmaking; EAF in particular is typically charged with high quantities of scrap. The scrap used here is assumed to be of similar quality as the sponge iron from DRI and would therefore require similar processing steps.

Consider a batch of scrap containing only clean 304 stainless steel. In terms of purity, the stainless steel contains high levels of alloying elements, notably Cr and Ni, and would therefore require much refining during steelmaking processes to achieve minimum purity requirements for generic crude steel. It is arguably much more preferable, both financially and environmentally, to produce secondary stainless steel using this batch of scrap – this shortens the refining process and eliminates the need for making alloying additions during secondary steelmaking.

Another example may be a batch of mixed steel scrap. This scrap contains high quantities of various impurities, making it extremely difficult to refine, and as the exact composition of is unknown, it is also not viable to make specific steels from this mixture. It may be more appealing to feed this batch of scrap into an EAF production of low-grade steel, where there is a higher tolerance for impurities.

As seen above, scraps of different compositions and qualities often have different optimal secondary production routes. It is crucial, in recycling of steels, or any other material, to distinguish between different batches of scrap, in order to reduce both the financial and environmental cost of recycling.

Recycling: sources of scrap

The quality of scrap largely depends on its origin. Broadly speaking, scrap can be categorised by their time of creation in material life cycle.

New scrap Scrap generated during production or manufacture is termed “new”. This type of scrap is typically well-known in terms of its composition and processing history, therefore can be re-introduced to the production process very efficiently.

New scrap generated at the production stage (in steel mills, etc.) is typically recycled in house – for example, crude steel produced by BOF is often immediately cast and rolled to sheets or formed into bars; trimmings from this process are a type of new scrap, and can be fed directly back into the BOF or an EAF facility in the same steel mill. The in-house recycling is often not accounted for when calculating recycled content.

New scrap generated outside of the production facility (in manufacturing factories, etc.) are sold back to production, either directly or through dealers; in the simplest case, this type of new scrap can be sold back to the original producers. In general, as scrap of well-known origin and composition is typically sold for higher prices, factories are often motivated to keep track of their recyclable waste.

Old scrap Once the product leaves the manufacturing stage, any scrap generated is known as old scrap. Most immediate example of old scrap is the waste in domestic recycling bins, which may contain a variety of materials with high levels of contamination; as a result, recycling rate for domestic scrap is typically fairly low.

Higher quality old scrap may include scrapped cars, end-of-life machinery parts, or construction waste – while the materials themselves may be low-grade, their origins are fairly well-known, hence it is easier to determine which type or grade of secondary material would be most suitably produced using these materials.

Going back to basics, from a recycling facility’s point of view, it is useful to compare grades of scrap and the secondary material they are used to produce.

Upgrading Provided the scrap used is suitable and different scraps of known compositions are mixed in correct ratios, higher-grade secondary material can (very occasionally) be produced. The material undergoes “upgrading” as a result of various impurities (alloying additions) in the different scrap mixing in ideal ratios and resulting in certain desirable properties.

Downgrading More often, however, the secondary material produced is typically of a lower grade compared to the scrap used in its production. “Downgrading” is largely a consequence of the mixed impurities and contamination in the scrap material. Unknown composition leads to unpredictably secondary material, and as mentioned in secondary steelmaking, refining can be a very expensive process, especially when the material contains many impurities.

In general, scrap of well-known compositions is preferred, as it limits the amount of downgrading; the following section discusses various technologies used by recycling centres to process mixed scrap, in order to maximise recycling efficiency and profit margins.

Recycling: scrap processing

The value of a batch of scrap material depends on the material type, particle size, and the overall scrap purity. A recycling centre may receive end-of-life products made of a variety of materials, and they are responsible for processing the waste to increase its overall value.

Separation

Different components of a product may need to be mechanically separated. This may be removing car seats from scrapped cars, or breaking apart old furniture to liberate any metal screws and nails.

Waste is commonly shredded to break apart different components. Compared to manual dismantling, shredders are much quicker and can handle heavier products. This process also reduces average particle size, which makes some sorting processes easier. Note however any fluids or hazardous parts need to be removed before a product can be recycled.

Granulators break apart small particles into even smaller granules. These are useful for removing rubber casing from electrical cables, etc.

Sorting

Sorting is often carried out on conveyor belts. The particles move through various facilities which test their properties.

Depending on physical properties, different materials may be reduced to different particle sizes after shredding. Size sorting can quickly and cheaply pick out brittle materials such as glass or ceramics.

Magnetic sorting tests magnetic response of scrap particles – ferrous metals are attracted to large magnets and separated from the rest. Similarly, eddy current sorting applies a magnetic field of varying strength, which, by magnetic induction and Lenz’s law, affects the motion of conducting particles (metals).

Sensors are also used in modern-day recycling. Different materials have different colours, as well as near infra-red (NIR, used to discern polymers) and X-ray fluorescence (XRF, used to determine elemental make-up) signals. The sensor communicates with a control system to sort the different particles into different piles; for example, an air gun is sometimes used to blow particles in different directions.

In many case, manual sorting remains the most effective method of sorting. Depending on the facility, manual sorting can be used to cherry-pick special parts (batteries, small motors, etc.) or carry out majority of the sorting process.

Pretreatment

Some impurities or contaminations can be reduced by pretreating the scrap. Examples include thermal treatment of aluminium scrap to remove coatings and contaminated surface layers, or electrolytic removal of tin coating from cans.

Summary

Primary production of steel describes the processes involved to produce steel from ores. This includes:

Ore processing Ores are first reduced to iron. This is commonly done in a blast furnace, where the furnace temperature is sufficiently high such that the iron is molten, or via direct reduction of iron (DRI), where the ores remain solid for the entire duration of reaction, and solid (sponge) iron is produced in the end.

Steelmaking Iron is further refined to produce steel. Primary steelmaking via blast oxygen furnace (BOF) or electric arc furnace (EAF) focuses on reducing C content in iron, which was mostly introduced during ore processing due to the use of C-containing reducing agents. High-grade steels, with more stringent requirements on impurity levels, undergoes secondary steelmaking processes, where the steel is further refined. Any required alloying elements are also added during steelmaking.

Waste may be generated at any point during a material life cycle. New scrap describes those generated during production and manufacture, while scrap from later on in the life cycle categorises as old scrap. For a given batch of scrap, its value and recycling efficiency is related to its purity. Old scrap is therefore sorted (at recycling centres, etc.) and different materials are recycled separately following their own recycling routes.

Steel scrap typically re-enters production at steelmaking stages, although the exact processes required depends on the scrap itself and the desired secondary product.

The overall environmental impacts of any product or material can be evaluated using life cycle analysis (LCA), which tracks energy use and relevant emissions for each stage of a life cycle, from product and manufacture to use and disposal. Designs which aim to reduce environmental impacts of steel production should study such impact with appropriate scope, in order to fairly evaluate overall effects of the design choices made.
